What this tool actually measures

A meow is not a flat sound: it is a short melody with a very specific shape. This page breaks the recording into 64-millisecond windows and extracts from each the fundamental frequency — pitch, measured in hertz — along with energy, period stability and the distribution of energy between low and high frequencies. From that time series it reconstructs the full melodic contour, counts syllables and calculates whether pitch rises or falls, how many semitones it spans and how much it wavers.

These are objective data: two people analysing the same recording get the same numbers. What is not objective is translating those numbers into intentions, which is why the tool always shows the measurements alongside the verdict, not just the verdict.

Cat sounds and what they mean

Meow

This is the vocalisation directed at people. Adult cats rarely meow at each other: the meow as we know it developed during domestication as a communication channel with humans. Its fundamental frequency typically falls between 300 and 900 Hz. Request meows tend to be longer with a rising ending; greeting meows are brief and high-pitched.

Purr

Occurs around 25–30 Hz, well below the meow range, and happens in both comfortable and uncomfortable or painful situations: it is not a reliable indicator that the cat is content. This tool does not detect it well because its pitch analysis starts at 180 Hz.

Chirp and chatter

That staccato sound cats make while watching birds through the window. It is brief, high-pitched and highly modulated, and appears linked to hunting excitement.

Hiss and growl

These are noisy sounds with no defined pitch: in the analysis they are recognised because harmonicity collapses and spectral flatness spikes. They signal that the cat feels threatened and needs distance. When a cat hisses, the effective response is to back off, not to persist.

Why is my cat meowing more than usual

A sudden change in the amount or type of vocalisations deserves attention. Common causes include hunger and poorly timed feeding routines, boredom from lack of stimulation, environmental changes (moving house, building works, a new animal), heat in unneutered animals and attention-seeking that we have inadvertently reinforced by always responding to meowing.

There are also medical causes: hyperthyroidism, hypertension, joint pain and cognitive decline in older cats all present with increased vocalisation, often at night. If the change is sudden, if the meow sounds different from usual, or if it is accompanied by weight loss, changes in appetite, drinking or litter box use, the right response is a vet visit, not an app. No acoustic tool replaces a physical examination.

Play and environmental enrichment

Much of the insistent meowing from an indoor cat is a request for stimulation. Feline play mimics the hunting sequence — stalking, chasing, catching, biting and disengaging — and works better in short, frequent sessions than in one long session per day. Two or three ten-minute rounds with a feather wand or fabric mouse that moves like prey (moving away, hiding, never toward the cat), always ending with a real catch it can bite, leave the animal much calmer than any automatic toy.

Passive enrichment matters equally: height for climbing and observing, boxes and hiding spots, vertical and horizontal scratching posts, a window perch, and puzzle feeders that require the cat to work for its food instead of eating from a bowl. Splitting the daily ration into several small meals often greatly reduces night-time meowing.

How to record for a good analysis

Get within one metre, record in a quiet room without background television, and capture the complete meow from just before it starts to just after it ends. Leave half a second of silence on each side: the analysis uses this to estimate background noise. Avoid the sound of hands rubbing the microphone, which saturates low frequencies and contaminates the measurements.

Scientific references

These are the sources supporting what this page describes, and also those explaining why the result is presented as a hypothesis rather than a translation. Almost all are open access.

  1. Nicastro, N. & Owren, M. J. (2003). Classification of domestic cat (Felis catus) vocalizations by naive and experienced human listeners. Journal of Comparative Psychology, 117(1), 44–52. doi.org/10.1037/0735-7036.117.1.44 The reference study on the limits of interpretation. Listeners identified the context above chance, but only modestly, with better results when hearing full sequences rather than single calls. Those who lived with cats performed better.
  2. Prato-Previde, E. et al. (2020). What's in a Meow? A Study on Human Classification and Interpretation of Domestic Cat Vocalizations. Animals, 10(12), 2390. doi.org/10.3390/ani10122390 Replicates the previous finding across three contexts: waiting for food, isolation and brushing. Recognition rates were low, better for the positive food context than for isolation.
  3. Schötz, S., van de Weijer, J. & Eklund, R. (2019). Melody matters: An acoustic study of domestic cat meows in six contexts and four mental states. Proceedings VIHAR 2019, London, 29–34. doi.org/10.7287/peerj.preprints.27926v1 From Lund University's Meowsic project. They analysed 780 meows from 40 cats and found that positive contexts tend toward rising pitch contours while negative contexts tend toward falling ones. This is the basis for measuring pitch slope in this page.
  4. Tavernier, C., Ahmed, S., Houpt, K. A. & Yeon, S. C. (2020). Feline vocal communication. Journal of Veterinary Science, 21(1), e18. doi.org/10.4142/jvs.2020.21.e18 Review of the complete vocal repertoire of the domestic cat: up to 21 distinct vocalisations have been described, and the authors suggest there are probably more.
  5. McComb, K., Taylor, A. M., Wilson, C. & Charlton, B. D. (2009). The cry embedded within the purr. Current Biology, 19(13), R507–R508. doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2009.05.033 Describes the solicitation purr: cats embed a high-pitched component resembling an infant cry, which is difficult for the human ear to ignore.
  6. Sissom, D. E. F., Rice, D. A. & Peters, G. (1991). How cats purr. Journal of Zoology, 223(1), 67–78. doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7998.1991.tb04749.x The classic work on the mechanism and frequency of purring, far below the meow range — hence why this page's analysis, starting at 180 Hz, does not detect it.
  7. Yeon, S. C. et al. (2011). Differences between vocalization evoked by social stimuli in feral cats and house cats. Behavioural Processes, 87(2), 183–189. doi.org/10.1016/j.beproc.2011.03.003 Compares domestic and feral cats in affiliative and conflict contexts, with differences in duration and frequency. Supports the idea of meowing as a behaviour directed at people.
  8. Ntalampiras, S. et al. (2019). Automatic Classification of Cat Vocalizations Emitted in Different Contexts. Animals, 9(8), 543. doi.org/10.3390/ani9080543 The most direct precedent for what this page attempts: automatic classification of meows by context based on acoustic features.

This site is not affiliated with any of these research groups, nor do their results endorse the classifier used here. The rule engine is inspired by trends described in the literature but does not reproduce any published method and has not been experimentally validated.

Frequently asked questions

Can you tell what a cat wants from its meow?

Only approximately. Feline bioacoustics studies suggest that meaning is largely individual: two cats can use nearly identical sounds for completely different things. That is why the tool includes a learning mode with labelled examples from your own cat — the only realistic path toward something close to accuracy.

What frequency is a cat meow?

The fundamental is typically between 300 and 900 Hz, with harmonics reaching several kilohertz. Kittens vocalise at higher pitches than adults.

Do cats meow at each other?

Very little. Between cats, body language, scent marking and sounds like hissing predominate. Meowing is directed almost exclusively at people.

Is the audio uploaded to any server?

No. All processing runs in the browser using the Web Audio API. The recording never leaves your device and disappears when you close the tab.

Does it work on iPhone?

Yes, in Safari on iOS 14.3 or later, provided the page is served over HTTPS. Safari blocks the microphone on pages opened as local files.
